The timing model

Copperline’s compatibility with cycle-sensitive software comes from one idea applied consistently: the chip bus is a single resource arbitrated per colour clock (CCK), and everyone pays for their slots. Reference numbers come from real hardware via the timing-test/ disk. The Copper and blitter timing models are documented in full below; the 68000 prefetch model is in CPU integration. Every rule here is backed by named regression tests (the inline suites in src/chipset/copper.rs and src/chipset/blitter.rs, plus src/bus/tests.rs and src/video/bitplane/tests.rs), and many by a golden probe render (see Cross-checking against hardware).

Custom-register read sampling¶

On the arbitrated 68000 path, CLXDAT is sampled and cleared at the start of its granted word transfer. Pixels clocked during that word or its trailing CPU clock belong to the next read. Paula INTREQR retains its independently calibrated end-of-slot sample. Byte reads select their lane; long reads sample each custom-bus word separately. Other registers retain their existing phases.

Live collision scans use the absolute pixel-comparator conversion x = 4 * H - 2 * DIW_HSTART_FB0, matching the window and sprite coordinates. The render-write anchor is a different domain and must not advance the collision cursor. At H=40thepixelcursorisx=60;DIWSTRT=40 the pixel cursor is x=60; DIWSTRT=40thepixelcursorisx=60;DIWSTRT=81 opens at x=62 during that clock. Reading CLXDAT at the transfer’s start must not include that later pixel. DMA grants, rendered positions and register-write events are unchanged.

Chip-bus arbitration¶

Every colour clock of every scanline has an owner, decided in priority order. The fixed-DMA levels (steps 1-5) are decided by fixed_dma_owner_at (src/bus/dma_slots.rs); Copper, blitter, and CPU contend for the slots it leaves free. (The function tests audio before disk, which is equivalent because their fixed slots never overlap.)

  1. Memory refresh -- fixed odd slots 1/3/5 plus the line-end refresh slot (E2, or E3 on NTSC long lines). The 1/3/5 slots sit on the odd parity the Copper never fetches from; the line-end slot lands on the Copper’s even-slot cadence but stalls only the CPU and blitter -- the refresh RGA strobe overlaps a concurrent Copper transfer on the real chip, so the arbiter lets a Copper fetch through it (line_end_refresh_slot in src/bus/dma_slots.rs). A Copper stream that saturates the line needs that clock: Nexus 7’s plasma-zoom text reloads 128+3 palette entries per 3-line band between two beam WAITs, and blocking E2 slips the chain ~6 cck per band until its BPLCON4 sprite-bank flip drifts into the sprite window.

  2. Disk DMA -- fixed slots 7/9/B, when DSKEN is set and a transfer is live.

  3. Audio DMA -- one fixed slot per Paula channel (D/F/11/13), claimed only when that channel actually has a fetch pending.

  4. Sprite DMA -- the fixed per-sprite slot pairs starting at 15/17.

  5. Bitplane DMA -- the fetch pattern determined by DDFSTRT/DDFSTOP and the plane count; at high plane counts this is what starves everyone else, exactly as on hardware.

  6. Copper -- even-CCK fetch cadence when not waiting.

  7. Blitter -- any remaining slots its schedule claims.

  8. CPU -- whatever is left.

For FMODE=0 fetches (OCS/ECS, and AGA with a 1-word fetch quantum) the bitplane decision (step 5) is driven by the Agnus DDF sequencer flop model (src/chipset/ddf_sequencer.rs, walked per line by src/bus/ddf_line.rs): DDFSTRT and DDFSTOP are comparator edges that set/clear flip-flops, not a value range. A stop request drains through one final fetch unit (which applies the modulos per plane), the hardwired window (18/18/18/D8) gates starts and forces stops, and the flop state carries across line boundaries. For a line with no mid-line sequencer writes, the walked fetch plan is keyed on the complete carried flop state, masked DDF registers, line geometry, chip revision and hard-stop mode. An identical key reuses the preceding line’s immutable slots and end state; the ordinary static walk is allocation-free. DDFSTRT/DDFSTOP/BPLCON0/DMACON/DIW writes invalidate that plan and rebuild the affected line (DDF writes commit to the comparators four colour clocks after the write slot; an old DDFSTOP still fires on its commit clock, an old DDFSTRT does not -- vAmiga’s sequencer semantics, hardware-verified in aggregate by the vAmigaTS Agnus/DDF/DDF/oldhwstop1-4 A500 photos). Because the complete initial state is in the key, a run carried across horizontal blanking or a vertical-window transition cannot accidentally reuse an ordinary interior line. A mid-row BPLCON0 change switches the fetch-unit slot layout from its commit clock; word addressing is unit-based, so late-enabled planes keep their word positions and earlier words stay zero.

The lo-res fetch unit is eight colour clocks with eight usable DMA slots. OCS/ECS Agnus drives six of them (slot order 4,6,2,3,5,1 at unit offsets 1,2,3,5,6,7), leaving offsets 0 and 4 free for the Copper/blitter/CPU -- which is why lo-res tops out at six bitplanes there. Alice drives those two remaining slots once BPLCON0 asks for more than six planes (plane 8 at offset 0, plane 7 at offset 4, giving the full order 8,4,6,2,7,3,5,1), so an eight-bitplane AGA lo-res screen leaves no spare bitplane slot in the unit.

Wide-FMODE (quantum > 1) fetches keep the memoized value-window plan: the effective DDF window, fetch cadence, and per-plane fetch-order mask live in a BitplaneSlotPlan keyed on the register inputs (BitplaneSlotKey, src/bus.rs). On a line without a fetch-affecting write, that plan is published as a complete per-line slot mask and is also shared with the DMA capture path for its stable cadence and row width. The colour-clock arbiter therefore performs a bit test, while capture does not re-derive the same DDF geometry on every quantum. A mid-line DDF, BPLCON0, DMACON, DIW or FMODE write invalidates the publication and selects the existing block-delay-aware calculation for the rest of that line. If a delayed BPLCON0 or DMACON change crosses the line boundary, the following line also remains dynamic until the delayed value has taken effect. A restored save state likewise keeps the remainder of its partial line dynamic, then republishes from the next unchanged line. Wide-FMODE lo-res slots are packed into the first eight CCKs of each 16/32-CCK fetch unit; the rest of the unit remains available to later arbitration priorities.

The value-window model still honours the DDF start comparator’s single-cycle match. A DDFSTRT write that moves the match position to the current colour clock or behind the beam, before the position it replaces has fired, leaves that line with no start at all: the counter never returns to the new value before the horizontal wrap, so the line fetches nothing and no bitplane pointer advances. A write landing after the flop has set cannot un-start the run -- only DDFSTOP ends it. Restarting the fetch from the moved comparator instead truncates the first unit, so only the planes whose lo-res slot number survives it fetch, and those pointers stay one wide-fetch word ahead of the rest for the remainder of the display. Microcosm’s CD32 status panel is the regression example: its Copper repoints seven bitplanes and drops DDFSTRT $2C to $18 in one burst that overruns the line, committing the new value at hpos ~$1E on the panel’s first line, which desynchronised BPL5 and BPL1 (lo-res slots 6 and 7) from the other five planes and speckled the whole panel. Covered by the ddfprobe-ddfmiss golden probe, verified against vAmiga 5.0b1’s A1200_2MB AGA setup.

Slow RAM at $C00000 is arbitrated through Agnus like chip RAM: a CPU access to slow RAM contends with DMA even though the RAM is outside the chip range. (Getting this wrong is observable: too-fast slow RAM can race a guest DMACON clear against the vertical-blank interrupt.) Fast RAM and ROM are external-bus accesses billed at the CPU clock without chip-bus arbitration -- so an accelerated CPU speeds up exactly what a real accelerator would.

The A1200’s 32-bit Alice path moves an aligned longword in one granted CPU slot, but the slot is not the complete latency of a read. A 68020 chip-RAM read, instruction-cache fill, or custom-register read waits one more CCK for the chipset data-return phase; a write can be posted after the 020’s shorter three-clock local-bus cycle. Sequential instruction words from the same aligned longword use the AGA fetch latch and do not request or wait for the same value twice. This read/write asymmetry is covered by aga_68020_chip_reads_wait_for_data_but_writes_are_posted.

Deferred timed-device ticks¶

The chipset and beam advance every colour clock, but the timed devices the CPU can only observe indirectly -- the CIAs, serial port, pots, audio, floppy, Akiko -- are not ticked per CPU bus access (that dominated the host profile). Instead their colour clocks accumulate in pending_device_tick and flush_timed_devices applies them in one batch at the next observation boundary: a CIA/custom/peripheral register read or write, or an instruction boundary for interrupt recognition. Read-only custom registers (INTREQR/DSKBYTR/SERDATR/POTxDAT) and writes that change device state (INTREQ/INTENA/ADKCON/DSKLEN/AUDxxx/SERDAT) flush first, so a device always reflects time right up to the moment it is read or written. The CIA E-clock divider and every device tick are exact under batching, so observable timing is unchanged -- the accumulator is a host-CPU optimisation only, and it is flushed to zero at every frame boundary (so it is never serialized into a save state).

CPU vs blitter¶

BLTPRI affects ownership only on colour clocks when the blitter actually requests the chip bus. A requested slot goes to the blitter ahead of the CPU; startup ladder cycles, the empty first-D bubble, line-mode Bresenham cycles, fill idle cycles, and disabled-channel gaps do not create a separate warm-up fence. The CPU can use those idle slots even when BLTPRI is set. With BLTPRI clear, the blitter still holds requested slots until the CPU has missed three consecutive chip-bus clocks, then yields one slot; fixed-DMA denials count among those misses. The starvation threshold and reset rule are described in CPU contention below.

This model is supported by chip-bus regressions and aligned One ROM captures, not title-specific timing assumptions. An independent colour-clock sequence model that gives BLTPRI precedence only when a blitter bus request is asserted reproduces every one of the 3,456 hardware priority-on busy samples. Across busy, IRQ and destination-memory observers, 3,456 aligned trials for each of the two BLTPRI settings match both hardware and WinUAE in masked state and elapsed colour clocks: 20,736 hardware trials total, with no mismatches. The priority-on busy and IRQ ROM source IDs are 93adfec44fe58ea38aba0e07e81f7949d1eaf519dc6bcc5914d10f2f5d2ccf1f and 18786132832d8aab99388ce1a1c52fbd12ff01465f40a73d21c71799f07edd53. Sprite DMA control rewrites¶

Sprite DMA fetches POS/CTL at a channel’s fixed pair slots on its vstop line, and DATA/DATB on the lines between vstart and vstop; the register-level channel model is described in Chipset modules. Standard hard vertical blank suppresses those fetches until PAL line 19orNTSCline19 or NTSC line19orNTSCline14, so SPRxPT writes made before that boundary set where the field’s first control-word fetch reads rather than retargeting words that could not yet have been fetched. Software can rewrite SPRxPOS/SPRxCTL while a sprite is pending, or before a later pair slot, to reposition an already active sprite on that scanline. Those writes update the channel’s register copies and vertical comparators (re-evaluated at once when they land on the matching line), but they leave SPRxPT alone: the data stream continues from the live pointer, and only the rewritten HSTART applies from that line. An SPRxPT write while a sprite is pending or armed retargets the next fetched data words, including for a sprite armed directly by CPU POS/CTL writes. The whole per-channel state is chip state and is serialized in save states. Tests: pending_sprite_control_rewrite_preserves_descriptor_data_origin, active_sprite_control_rewrite_preserves_descriptor_data_origin, pending_descriptor_sprite_pointer_write_retargets_data_stream, after_slot_armed_sprite_pointer_write_seeds_dma_data_stream.

Beam-timed SPRxPOS writes are replayed in Denise’s horizontal-comparator domain, seven colour clocks ahead of the normal register-output position (SPRITE_REGISTER_WRITE_PIPELINE_CCK in src/video/bitplane.rs). Copper writes use three, which takes back out the four-clock WAIT-comparator lookahead already in the Copper’s landings. This matters for manual sprite reuse with sprite DMA disabled: Copper lists can write consecutive SPRxPOS values whose HSTARTs exactly abut. SPRxDATA and SPRxDATB writes update Denise’s data latches at their ordinary beam position, but the sprite serializer copies those latches only when the horizontal comparator fires. A same-line DATA/DATB write after that compare therefore waits for a later compare or scanline instead of replacing the word already shifting. Same-line POS/CTL writes also do not truncate a manual sprite word that has already started shifting; they re-arm a later compare while the active word completes. Tests: manual_sprite_position_write_before_hstart_uses_sprite_compare_domain, manual_sprite_position_writes_use_denise_compare_lag, manual_sprite_position_write_does_not_truncate_started_word, manual_sprite_data_write_after_compare_waits_for_next_scanline, sprite_register_data_write_after_compare_preserves_dma_latch_on_same_beam_line.

The Copper¶

The Copper (src/chipset/copper.rs) is a two-cycle processor that accesses chip RAM on every other colour clock. The HRM describes MOVE as a two-word, two-memory-cycle instruction; Copperline models the cadence and its edge cases in detail.

The fetch cadence and the MOVE write boundary¶

The two-CCK cadence is locked to the beam’s horizontal parity, not a free-running internal phase: the Copper fetches on even in-line colour clocks and idles on the odd ones (Copper::hpos_is_access_cycle). So:

  • A MOVE spans four colour clocks (fetch, idle, fetch, idle), leaving the alternate clocks free for the blitter/CPU. Modelling the Copper as owning every clock while running would starve a chip-bus-bound CPU during dense Copper effects such as horizontal colour gradients.

  • WAIT and SKIP span eight colour clocks, spending a two-Copper-cycle tail after their two word fetches (the Minimig FETCH1/FETCH2/WAITSKIP1/WAITSKIP2 sequence; vAmiga COP_WAIT1/COP_WAIT2 at fetch2+2 and fetch2+4): an immediately-true WAIT or a SKIP resumes fetching at fetch2+6.

  • The custom-register side effect occurs on the second word fetch, i.e. the third of the four colour clocks: three back-to-back MOVEs starting at beam hpos write at hpos + 2, hpos + 6, and hpos + 10.

  • The WAIT comparator’s horizontal input runs two colour clocks ahead of the beam (CopperWait::comparator_is_satisfied), wrapping through 0 over the last three clocks of a line. A sleeping WAIT therefore wakes at target-2 -- the match colour clock is the bus-free wake-up cycle -- and the next instruction’s first fetch lands exactly on the masked target, its write two clocks later. Cross-verified against vAmiga with the two-sided landing probes (COPPERLINE_DIAG_COP_WRITES / VAMIGA_COP_PROBE): a WAIT $4721 + MOVE SPR0CTL lands the write at hpos $22 in both emulators (vAmigaTS spritedma/interfere2, matching the real-A500 photo).

  • The wake-up itself is a real Copper cycle: it must land on an access-parity colour clock that fixed DMA (bitplane/sprite/disk/audio/ refresh) leaves free (vAmiga’s COP_REQ_DMA reschedules until the bus is free). On a quiet bus this is invisible -- the match clock doubles as the wake-up. Inside a 5/6-plane lores or hires fetch window, where bitplanes own even colour clocks too, the wake-up is pushed to the next free slot and the woken MOVE’s fetches slip one free slot further (a 6-plane lores group leaves only offsets 0 and 4 free, so the write lands a full slot later than the quiet-bus spacing). Shadow of the Beast’s title relies on this: its per-line WAIT (v,$40) / MOVE COLOR00 toggles tuck against the display window edge at colour clock 48onlybecausethewake−upconsumesthefreeslotat48 only because the wake-up consumes the free slot at 48onlybecausethewake−upconsumesthefreeslotat40 (ddfprobe-sotb/-sotb2 pin the quiet-bus and starved landings against vAmiga; copper_wait_wakeup_spends_a_free_slot_under_lores6_fetch).

Anchoring the cadence to the beam rather than to a carried-over flip-flop is what makes a back-to-back colour MOVE list land its writes at the same hpos on every line. With a free-running phase, fixed-DMA interference drifts the phase line-to-line and a Copper-driven horizontal gradient shimmers instead of showing clean vertical bands. The parity check is applied by Copper::step_eligible_slot, the single primitive shared by the live bus path and the blitter-deadline predictor’s cloned simulation, so prediction and execution cannot drift apart.

When the 68k is halted in STOP, its idle fast-forward path may encounter a Copper in the steady comparator phase of WAIT. It computes that WAIT’s exact beam/blitter deadline once and leaves the comparator dormant strictly before the deadline while the ordinary DMA arbiter continues to own every colour clock. The shortcut is capped at the field wrap because the vertical-blank COP1LC strobe supersedes a wait that would otherwise extend into the next field. Instruction-tail and wake-up cycles remain individually stepped.

Ordinary CPU-driven advances use the same invariant through a cached bound (Bus::copper_sleeping_before_wake_bound, consulted by every bus quantum): once a WAIT is in its steady comparator phase, the earliest colour clock at which it could release -- the nearest of its position, a pending frame restart and the field wrap, with the end-of-list WAIT bounded by the wrap alone -- is computed once and kept as an absolute clock. Quanta strictly before it leave the comparator dormant; at it, the comparator runs every eligible slot again and wakes the Copper at the slot it always would. A WAIT already at its position, including one held by a running blitter, gets no bound: the wake slot and the blitter-finished condition stay with the per-clock path. The bound is dropped by whatever can move the wake -- a COPJMP strobe, VPOSW/VHPOSW, a DMACON write that changes the Copper’s own DMA gate, a sleep/wake transition, reset -- and is never saved.

Register writes take effect a fixed number of colour clocks after the chip-bus slot that carried them, and the delay is a property of the register pipeline, not of the bus master. Denise-boundary registers apply to the pixel pipeline about four colour clocks after the slot (DENISE_WRITE_EFFECT_DELAY_CCK; vAmiga: a register-change delay plus pixel-domain application offsets). Agnus’s two-cycle register class (DMACON, BPLxPT, BPLxMOD, SPRxPT; vAmiga recordRegisterChange(DMA_CYCLES(2))) applies two colour clocks after the slot (AGNUS_WRITE_EFFECT_DELAY_CCK); the bitplane/sprite DMA-gating replay is calibrated against events recorded at that position. Render events are recorded at the effective position for every writer: a Copper MOVE executes at its bus slot, so its event records at the current beam position plus the delay; a CPU write is applied once its whole bus cycle has been billed (the beam sits past the granted slot by then), so its event is referenced from the granted slot itself (Bus::cpu_custom_access_slot) plus the same delay (Bus::record_render_write). WAIT/SKIP edge cases¶

The Copper compares its masked beam position against the Agnus beam counters; the BFD bit controls whether a satisfied position wait must also wait for the blitter to go idle.

  • Full-mask waits are satisfied once the beam reaches or passes the target in the current 8-bit vertical phase. Near the end of PAL line 255, low-half targets wait for the short post-rollover tail while high-half targets (e.g. $fc) stay satisfied because that phase has passed; partial-mask waits with the high vertical bit set likewise stay satisfied across the line-255-to-256 rollover.

  • With BFD clear a position-satisfied wait parks while the blitter is busy and resumes once the scheduled blit consumes its slots; with BFD set it ignores a busy blitter.

  • The WAIT comparator cannot release in the last 4 colour clocks of a line (WAIT_RELEASE_LINE_END_BLACKOUT_CCK); a wait satisfied there releases at its target on a following line, unless its compare position lies inside the blackout (the only clock where it is ever true), which still releases there.

  • The comparator is combinational and runs on every colour clock, including ones owned by fixed DMA (bitplane/sprite/disk/audio/refresh); bus contention only delays the next fetch, never the release decision. The CDTV extended-ROM boot list depends on this: its WAIT vp=$FF hp=$DE is only releasable at hpos DEofline255,whichsitsinsidethelastDDFSTOP=DE of line 255, which sits inside the last DDFSTOP=DEofline255,whichsitsinsidethelastDDFSTOP=D8 overscan fetch unit -- evaluating the comparator only on Copper-eligible slots lost the release and the follow-up display-off MOVE. Tests: wait_release_is_blocked_in_line_end_blackout, copper_wait_comparator_runs_under_fixed_bitplane_dma.

COPJMP and frame reload¶

  • A CPU write to COPJMP1/COPJMP2 loads the Copper program counter immediately, but the target list has no visible effect until the Copper gets DMA slots to fetch it.

  • A CPU read of COPJMP1/COPJMP2 fires the same strobe: the register decode acts on the address alone (reading a write-only register performs a bus write of the floating data-bus residue into it -- the UAE/vAmiga model), while the CPU reads back the undriven bus. Vertical-blank handlers rely on this (move.l #list,COP1LC + tst.w COPJMP1) to make a double-buffered list swap take effect in the same frame, before display fetch begins (regression example: the Sleepwalker CD32 logo/intro double-buffer flip, which without the read strobe shows each new pose one frame early in the still-displayed buffer). Test: copjmp_strobe_fires_on_read_access; golden probe: timing-test/copprobe-jmpread.asm (vAmiga-verified).

  • A Copper MOVE can update COP1LC/COP2LC; a later COPJMP strobe branches through the current value. A Copper MOVE to COPJMP1/COPJMP2 spends its second word fetch on the strobe, then two more bus-free Copper cycles (vAmiga COP_JMP1/COP_JMP2); the program counter reloads on the second of those, so the first fetch from the new list lands three Copper cycles after the strobe (verified against the vAmiga copper trace: a COPJMP2 MOVE at hpos 04fetchesthetargetlist′sfirstMOVEat04 fetches the target list's first MOVE at 04fetchesthetargetlist′sfirstMOVEat0A, its write landing at $0C).

  • The automatic frame reload latches the current COP1LC at end of frame and restarts the Copper at the top of the next frame (vpos 0) through the vertical-blank lines -- it branches through a Copper-programmed COP1LC value, so a MOVE to COP1LC changes where the next frame restarts. The blitter¶

The blitter (src/chipset/blitter.rs) is not a do-it-all-at-once engine with a delay: it is scheduled per DMA slot. Each word issues its hardware channel bus sequence, so DMACONR’s BBUSY reflects genuine in-flight state and BBUSY-polling loops see hardware timing. Because the renderer and the CPU both need to know when a blit finishes, cck_until_blitter_completes (src/bus/dma_slots.rs) predicts completion by walking the same slot-eligibility primitive the live engine uses.

Per-slot FSM¶

Scheduled blits use explicit phases for the hardware controller. ROM-resident One ROM probes on a PAL A500 (ECS Agnus, OCS Denise, 512 KB chip + 512 KB slow) separately observe busy, interrupt and destination-memory reads. Launch phases are aligned before starting the blit; the observer’s first chip-bus access is the measured read, followed by a raw beam timestamp. Across all normal USE masks, eight D-enabled fill masks and two line modes, the busy, IRQ and memory observers cover 20,736 trials across BLTPRI clear and set. Hardware, WinUAE Best Compatibility and Copperline match in observed state and elapsed read time. DMACONR.BZERO is measured separately: three clocks of normal propagation, plus one for fill’s USE-D/no-USE-C idle phase, match the complete 4,608-row packet byte-for-byte. Broader line-mode flag semantics remain unresolved. The measurement report and remaining plan records the scope and comparison baselines. The measurements refine the earlier cross-emulator slot traces:

  • Startup: CPU-launched normal-mode StartDelay and Init each consume a fixed-DMA-free colour clock after the BLTSIZE write completes. They do not claim the bus and can advance alongside Copper transfers. A500 source-overwrite races distinguish the first-stage stall under six-plane DMA from the second-stage stall under four-plane DMA. Copper-launched blits retain the separately calibrated deferred register pipeline and internal startup; line startup also retains its internal timing. Adding extra register-commit clocks here double-counted time already advanced by the CPU register-write bus cycle.

  • Body: the source cadence follows the enabled-channel speed table: A is always visited, B and C only when enabled, and D when D is enabled or no C next-word state exists. D output is delayed through the hold register: the first D phase of every program is the HRM “-” bubble and does not claim the chip bus -- including D-only clears, which write “-- D0 -- D1 | -- D2” on hardware. The first destination word is written on the next D slot and the final word in the F flush slot.

  • Termination: the last body cycle is followed by E (internal flush) and F (the final D write when enabled). BBUSY clears when E completes; the engine remains active until F completes. The interrupt is armed at E’s completion and asserted three colour clocks later, independently of a stalled F write. The bus countdown starts at four because the arming clock itself decrements it once. A chip-RAM CPU read samples its own granted slot before the trailing bus-cycle clock can perform a blitter write. Reading RAM after that tail incorrectly returned a destination word which hardware had not yet exposed to the CPU. Custom-register readback retains its separate timing path. The register file is released when BBUSY drops, so terminal cycles retire the queued D word without overwriting registers programmed for the next blit.

Normal-mode A/B barrel-shifter carry is cleared at the first word of a new BLTSIZE, then carries from the last source word of one row into the first source word of the next inside that blit; masks, modulos, and fill carry still observe row boundaries.

Normal A/B sources are read from live chip RAM in their granted DMA slots, just like C. BLTSIZE latches registers, not the contents of future source addresses: A500 kind77 copies a CPU overwrite made after launch when the CPU wins before the source fetch. An already fetched word remains latched for the shifter/minterm pipeline. D-to-A/B feedback and physical aliases therefore follow live RAM too. Legacy snapshot/overlay fields remain loadable in saved pending blits, but do not determine source data; new blits allocate no source snapshot buffers.

Line blits follow vAmiga’s four line micro-programs, indexed by the USEB/USEC pair: with USEB clear each pixel is four cycles (L1-L4: L2 fetches C when USEC is set, L3 propagates, L4 stores), and with USEB set each pixel is six (an LB cycle fetches B -- adding only BLTBMOD to BLTBPT -- and a bare LBus cycle allocates the bus without a transfer). With USEC set the line D cycle allocates the bus even when SING suppresses the store (unlike copy mode, where a locked D slot is bus-idle); with USEC clear no line cycle touches the bus, and the terminal BLTDONE cycle of a USEB program is itself a bus-idle cycle. A SING-suppressed dot only locks the store: the A shifter, minterm and BZERO update still run on the full inputs.

One ROM measurements on the PAL A500 additionally show a register-texture (USEB clear) BZERO evaluation after the texture counter wraps from BSH=0 to 15 between pixels. The model evaluates the preceding pixel’s shifted A and C holds with B=$ffff; it does not write a D word. That B input fits the earlier selected cases, but is not a universal hardware rule: the one-hot and minterm controls find repeatable exceptions at effective A bits 13 and 11, despite matching destination data and direct-A flag controls. L2 evaluates those old holds before fetching the next C word, and L3 exposes the queued clear. A blit starting at BSH=15 has no preceding pixel and does not trigger this check. Save states preserve the prior A hold and pending clear. The wrap-stage input/reduction exceptions, B-DMA and other non-wrap effects remain unresolved; see the One ROM measurement report.

The Bresenham error accumulator (BLTAPT’s low word) advances only while USEA is enabled, the line texture register is BLTBDAT rotated by the LIVE BSH each pixel (no write-time latch, unlike the USEB-off copy hold word), and C DMA fetches load the BLTCDAT register itself, so a later USEC-off blit consumes the last fetched C word. At completion the hardware-visible ASH, BSH, SIGN, and low-word BLTAPT accumulator state is written back. Verified cycle-for-cycle against the vAmiga line traces (bususage1l/5l/15l): both emulators run the pixel micro-cycles, bus grants and stalls on the same colour clocks relative to the BLTSIZE poke. Tests: scheduled_normal_mode_bbusy_start_delay_precedes_first_source_slot, blit_pipeline_identifies_idle_cycles_per_hrm_diagrams, scheduled_normal_clear_writes_progressively, scheduled_line_mode_latches_c_source_before_store_phase, scheduled_shift_carry_crosses_normal_mode_row_boundary, scheduled_a_shift_zero_fills_first_word_of_new_blit.

B DMA also updates raw BLTBDAT and the retained B shifter hold at each fetch, so a later operation with USEB clear consumes the last fetched/shifted data. One ROM A500 controls distinguish the line texture used to select a pixel from the hold retained for a subsequent normal-mode blit. With B DMA disabled, the first pixel retains a zero-filled shift of the raw word; subsequent pixels retain the pre-rotation texture. B-DMA lines retain the post-rotation texture instead. Line completion preserves the previous raw B word as carry for a later CPU BLTBDAT write using the then-current BSH. These updates occur on fetch/pixel events, so terminal drain does not overwrite a newer CPU write. The follow-up measurements record the tested patterns, shifts, lengths and write orders. This corrects retained data; the separate inter-pixel BZERO exceptions remain unresolved.

Micro-cycle stall classes¶

Non-bus blitter cycles come in two hardware classes, mirrored from vAmiga’s micro-instructions and encoded as BlitSlotClass::BusFree and BlitSlotClass::Internal (bus-access cycles are BlitSlotClass::Bus):

  • Bus-free cycles (vAmiga BUSIDLE: the D pipeline bubble, area fill’s extra idle cycle, the BLT_STRT startup cycles, a line blit’s internal Bresenham cycles) advance only on colour clocks the blitter could have won: they stall while the Copper or fixed DMA owns the clock, and on the clock a starved CPU is granted (the BLS line blocks busIsFree). They never allocate the bus, so the CPU can use the same colour clock on an uncontended access.

  • Internal cycles (vAmiga NOTHING: the BLTSIZE register commit, the micro-program begin cycle, the terminal D-hold flush and a D-less BLTDONE) elapse on every colour clock regardless of bus ownership.

Verified cycle-for-cycle against the vAmiga slot traces: a Copper-poked blit on a 6-plane display line stalls its BLT_STRT cycles through the display fetches and lands its first A fetch on the same colour clock in both emulators.

CPU contention¶

With BLTPRI clear, Copperline models the Agnus blitter-slowdown counter (the Minimig RTL bls_cnt). A busy “nice” blitter holds every slot in which its pipeline requests the chip bus; the CPU receives no regular alternate slot. Each consecutive chip-bus clock that denies the waiting CPU advances the counter, including fixed-DMA denials during a disabled-channel gap or pipeline bubble. An idle blitter phase does not itself reset the counter; a CPU grant does. After BLITTER_SLOWDOWN_CPU_MISS_LIMIT (3) misses, the blitter yields one slot, matching the HRM “one bus cycle in four” rule and the Minimig RTL. BLTPRI disables this starvation yield.

With BLTPRI set, the blitter wins only a slot where its current micro-cycle requests a bus access. Idle startup and pipeline cycles remain available to the CPU, including startup-ladder cycles and the empty first-D bubble. Once running, fill’s explicit idle cycle and line-mode BUSIDLE likewise do not request the bus. The aligned One ROM busy, IRQ and memory observer captures match hardware, WinUAE and Copperline for both BLTPRI settings, across 3,456 trials per observer and setting. The separate kind-22 BZERO matrix also matches the tested normal/fill readback; line-texture controls expose additional flag behavior that remains under investigation. The historical game-specific examples are removed as evidence for the rule.

A 68000 TAS read-modify-write is unsafe on chip RAM (the HRM warns against it); the m68k backend exposes it as a byte read then a byte write, so Copperline models TAS against chip RAM as two separately Agnus-arbitrated accesses, each subject to the same back-pressure rule. Tests: blithog_clear_busy_blitter_yields_to_cpu_only_after_starvation, bltpri_cpu_uses_first_d_bubble_but_waits_for_bus_accesses, bltpri_line_blit_bresenham_cycles_stay_cpu_available, bltpri_leaves_startup_and_first_d_bubble_available_to_cpu.

Area fill¶

Area fill is applied only when BLTCON1.DESC is set (the HRM requires descending mode because the fill carry propagates in descending bit order across each row); IFE/EFE in ascending mode is treated as ordinary minterm output. With USEC clear, fill adds one extra idle cycle (no bus access) per word, placed AFTER the D slot -- the trailing “-” in vAmiga’s fill micro-programs for USE masks 1/5/9/D (“A0 -- -- A1 D0 -- A2 D1 --”). So an A->D area fill costs 3 CCK/word vs 2 for an A->D copy. The fill carry datapath (apply_fill in finish_source_word) is not what adds the cycle -- the FillIdle slot in the controller sequence is. Cross-emulator validated: FS-UAE and vAmiga both time the bltcon0=0x09F0 A->D fill at 3 CCK/word (timing-test rows 23/24/26). USEC-carrying fills reuse their real C cycle and D-less fills match their copy timing (vAmiga’s fill programs change only masks 1/5/9/D). The idle fill phase advances on CPU/Copper/idle arbitration slots but not through fixed DMA (bitplane/sprite/disk/audio/refresh) slots. (An earlier experiment dropped the extra cycle to improve one capture, but that made the blitter faster than hardware and broke a separate blitter-heavy regression.) Test: descending_area_fill_costs_one_extra_idle_cycle_per_word.

ECS registers¶

BLTCON0L, BLTSIZV, and BLTSIZH are active only on an ECS Agnus. BLTSIZV latches the 15-bit vertical size but does not start the blit; BLTSIZH latches the 11-bit horizontal size and is the ECS start strobe; zero decodes to the documented maxima (32768 lines, 2048 words). BLTCON1.DOFF suppresses destination writes while still running the D channel timing, advancing the D pointer, and updating BZERO from the generated D data. Interrupt-recognition latency¶

A 68000 does not enter an exception the moment INTREQ rises. Copperline separates source recognition, instruction poll qualification and register readback:

  1. Each newly asserted source receives an independent recognition deadline (DEFAULT_IRQ_LATENCY_CCK = 6, src/bus.rs). The default is calibrated against the combined A500 board/CPU STOP path, rather than a direct measurement of Paula’s IPL pins. Later sources cannot postpone an earlier deadline. CPU mask/acknowledge writes update the bookkeeping; unmasking a latched PORTS level retains its existing immediate behavior. A new Copper-written PORTS request is a source assertion, not an INTENA unmask, and receives a recognition deadline.

  2. A running stock 68000 samples candidates at bus-access starts. Its ordinary qualified prefetch poll uses the deadline minus two CCK; a core-marked unqualified operand poll uses the deadline minus four CCK. The core holds the selected sample through subsequent accesses. Memory TST polls on its operand word (the low word for TST.L); DIVS can mark an internal poll before its final prefetch. Other CPU models and accelerated bus ratios retain their previous qualification. STOP uses the unchanged deadline directly and preserves its two-CCK polling grid. A cleared source is not delivered from a stale sample. COPPERLINE_IRQ_LATENCY_CCK=0 selects the raw model.

  3. INTREQR is the raw Paula latch, independent of the recognition deadline. A 68000 CPU read captures it during its granted transfer, before the bus cycle’s trailing CCK. Reading after the tail incorrectly included Copper writes that occurred after the data transfer. Long accesses sample the INTREQR word in its own slot. Other custom registers retain their separately calibrated phases, and the shorter 020+ data-return path is unchanged.

The focused One ROM measurements match all 2,212 A500 observations after these corrections; the earlier 2,560-divider and 896-STOP result packets also match hardware byte-for-byte. WinUAE Best Compatibility agrees on the same measured fields. This closes the observed poll/readback differences in those suites, not every instruction, source, DMA or priority transition. The previous full six-CCK qualification at a prefetch’s start counted its trailing transfer twice, making ordinary instruction recognition two CCK late. Globally shortening the deadline fixed those instructions but broke the already accurate STOP path.

Copper-generated PORTS/EXTER requests use the same running-instruction poll qualification as internal Paula requests. The request bit alone cannot identify an external CIA edge: a Copper INTREQ write can set either bit. A serialized origin mask preserves that distinction until acknowledgement. Staged CIA inputs also carry an origin tag and use the common running poll phase. Other external devices and unmeasured CPU-written external request paths retain their previous qualification. Paired A500 STOP/running/readback controls and remaining CIA request-visibility differences are recorded in the sibling probe repository at docs/irq-stage-investigation-2026-10-19.md. These measurements do not justify removing the six-clock default or claiming an isolated IPL-pin delay.

The six-CCK default is calibrated against amiga-hardware-probes captures from a PAL A500 with 512 KB chip RAM, 512 KB slow RAM, ECS Agnus and OCS Denise. With only Copper DMA enabled, a WAIT at line 64 followed by a MOVE that sets SOFTINT wakes a STOPped 68000: the handler’s first instruction records VHPOSR 37 CCK after the WAIT target at all sixteen horizontal phases, twice each. The previous five-CCK default recorded 36. This validates the combined Copper/interrupt/CPU/readback path, not an isolated measurement of the propagation delay at Paula’s pins.

CIA output phase and raw request visibility¶

The CIA ICR latch, external output, Paula request latch and CPU recognition are distinct observations. The CIA keeps its timer/readback logic on the E-clock grid. The bus forwards an ordinary timer assertion at the following falling phase (two CCK after the rising-edge divider event). An assertion delayed by an ICR-read race uses the next rising phase instead. The common one-E delayed-output path also uses this phase for mask-enable, TOD, FLAG and serial assertions. Electrical phase measurements establish the timer and ICR-race behavior; applying the same phase to these other delayed sources is an extrapolation, not an independently measured external-source latency. Settled SP/TOD/FLAG checks verify acknowledgement order only. This preserves the measured approximately six-CPU-clock difference between ordinary and race-delayed output, without delaying ICR flags by another whole E-clock.

Paula receives the modeled output through a separate four-CCK input stage. INTREQR remains clear before that stage completes. An ICR acknowledgement cancels an unforwarded assertion; once forwarded, Paula retains its own latch until INTREQ acknowledgement. A held input does not restart its input delay. Absolute event deadlines preserve these stages across batched device advances, and pending stages bound stopped-CPU advances.

The first CIA-fed request qualifies two CCK before the generic recognition reference: four CCK after raw-request visibility at the unchanged six-CCK default. A running stock 68000 uses the same qualified two/four-CCK poll leads as other Paula requests, while STOP uses the boundary deadline. A serialized CIA origin mask persists through CIA ICR acknowledgement until INTREQ is cleared; unrelated INT2/INT6 devices keep their former qualification. This is a CIA-route correction, not a global reduction. The output, input and recognition stages cannot be collapsed into one delay: consuming the source/input span makes STOP bus activity early; adding the full generic delay after input makes it late. The selected reference fits both STOP-entry phase groups and retains the software loader regression. Other Paula interrupt routes and their existing polling rules are unchanged.

STOP fast-forwarding also preserves the exact predicted device horizon after converting it to an instruction budget. For example, a three-CCK event must not advance four CCK because that budget rounded up. Device and slice accounting use the bounded advance; already-executed bus time is never rewound. This does not force CPU recognition at the device event: the STOP polling grid still governs acceptance. With the staged CIA route above, the exact bound repairs 33 phase-qualified A500 STOP-entry beam observations while preserving running and masked controls and the ECS/OCS loader regression. Applying this scheduler change to the earlier propagation model had broken that loader; the two stages must be validated together.

The sibling probe repository retains the exact ROMs’ source fingerprints, original four-channel captures and comparison rules in references/cia-recognition-20261024-v1/. The masked read controls return clear in the approximately 1.06–3.53 CPU-clock /AS window after CIA /IRQ and set in the 5.06–7.53 window. Physical /IPL follows /IRQ by about 8 CPU clocks. These are observed bus intervals, not direct measurements of an internal CPU poll or an exact transistor-level Paula latch. Full beam/refresh cohorts remain incomplete; do not count observed-range agreement as an exact timing match.

CIA external-input forwarding and CPU qualification have distinct reference points; changing INTREQR’s common read phase also changes other Paula sources. The input/qualification split preserves the combined CPU deadline while keeping INTREQR’s existing end-of-slot read sampling for internal and external sources. Before a CPU IPL poll, flush a deferred device batch only when an enabled CIA input is already due for forwarding. This applies elapsed device time without advancing the CPU clock; otherwise the batch can hide a request from a running instruction even though the STOP scheduler observes it. Future requests remain pending and use the ordinary qualification deadline. Keep external serial traffic conditioned separately: an attached keyboard’s synchronization bits can set CIA-A’s SP flag even without keypresses. Hardware comparisons assume an attached keyboard; condition its startup traffic before comparing CIA readback.

A subsequent RAM-resident ROM probe on the same A500 measured both 37 and 38 CCK with DMA disabled. A fixed WAIT at line 96, horizontal 64, and a NOP sweep before STOP separated the CPU phase from the Copper target. Across 256 trials, the parity of the logged entry beam predicted the result exactly; inserting EXG (six CPU clocks) reversed the relationship. The stock 68000 STOP path therefore retains a four-CPU-clock (two-CCK) polling grid from STOP entry and recognizes a newly visible IPL at the next poll. This phase is saved with the machine. The boundary-recognition setting remains six CCK; changing that constant cannot reproduce both results.

On the 68000/68010 motherboard bus, interrupt acknowledge reads the low byte of the ROM word at $FFFFF0 + 2 * level. Normal Kickstart’s final eight words contain vector numbers 24 through 31. A custom ROM must supply these words; Copperline does not replace a missing word with an autovector or suppress delivery because a RAM handler is zero. The freezer cartridge retains its own acknowledge behavior. The accelerated CPU bus path retains autovector delivery. This ROM-data behavior was checked on the One ROM A500 by changing the level-1 word to select the installed vector-30 handler. The zero-footer measurement ROM now reproduces the physical machine’s illegal-instruction fault at $003020, rather than completing its trials.

CIA register transfers and timer startup¶

A CIA access uses the 68000’s synchronous VPA/E bus cycle, not an ordinary four-clock DTACK cycle (MC68000 User’s Manual, appendix B). The E period is ten CPU clocks, six low and four high. cpu_cia_access synchronizes the transfer to the E falling edge. The register transfer occurs before that edge’s CIA update; cpu_cia_access_finish applies the edge and then advances the final six CPU clocks. Applying a START write after its transfer edge would miss the first timer pipeline stage and postpone underflow by an entire E-clock. The total access cost is unchanged.

Timers A and B propagate START through two count-enable stages on PHI2. Clearing START reads back immediately, but counting continues for the two edges while those stages drain. A STOP-only delay sweep measured two extra decrements on both timers of both physical CIAs (320 trials). One-shot underflow stops counting directly. START+LOAD, including a stopped one-shot timer’s high-byte auto-start, holds counting for three E-clock edges. FORCE LOAD with START still set applies the same three-edge hold to an already-running timer; it does not require a stopped-to-running transition. A One ROM sweep on the physical A500 measured this on both timers of both CIAs: 640 reload trials, five launch phases and sixteen read delays. Before the correction every live sample was three counts too low; afterwards both the live and fully stopped counts matched all 640 hardware samples exactly. This also removes the three-E-clock bias in repeated timing-test stopwatch rows, whose one-shot high-byte write starts the timer before CRA FORCE LOAD. On a CPU VPA write the transfer edge feeds the first stage. The timer’s START bit is readable during startup; one-shot underflow still clears it. Startup advances on PHI2 even for CNT/cascade modes, and underflow predictions include the remaining stages so a stopped CPU cannot nap past an interrupt. The LOAD holds and count-enable stages travel in snapshots. Older snapshots reconstruct the stages from their running flag and startup delay; legacy USS imports restore a steady running or stopped timer.

The clock-source and one-shot qualifiers also pass through two PHI2 stages. Changing a running timer B from PHI2 to timer-A underflows retains two final PHI2 decrements; changing back delays the first PHI2 decrement by two edges. Clearing one-shot together with START does not cancel a one-shot underflow already in those stages. The qualifier history travels in snapshots.

Timer-A underflows reach timer B’s cascade input one E-clock later. The pending final pulse is saved and included in STOP wakeup predictions, even when timer A has already stopped. Batched updates retain that final pulse and deliver all preceding ones; latch zero produces one underflow per enabled input tick. One-shot batches normally stop at their first underflow; the zero-latch high-byte auto-start exception is described below. A zero START+LOAD also produces carry on the last LOAD stage, before the first decrement, and can stop a one-shot there.

CIA batches step individual PHI2 edges while timer PB outputs, serial output, an ICR-read race, or control pipelines need edge ordering. This preserves PB toggle parity and one-edge pulse widths, and prevents elapsed batch time from consuming the IRQ-pin delay of a serial completion on the final edge. Once these effects settle, ordinary timer counting remains batched. Regression tests compare complete states after identical clock sequences partitioned into one batch or individual edges. This is a batching consistency correction, not a new measurement of physical IRQ propagation or a latency recalibration.

Physical A500 captures from 19 October 2026 additionally constrain the Timer-A PB6 and serial output behavior on both CIAs. Pulse mode idles low and drives high for the underflow pulse, including when DDRB selects input. START initializes the toggle latch high. Adjacent zero-latch underflows merge into one asserted pulse and therefore produce only one toggle edge; a gap rearms the next edge. High-byte one-shot auto-start also initializes the toggle latch. Automatic high-byte start can carry from the old stopped counter before the new count’s underflow. A500 kind133 crosses old count 0/1/3/15 with new latch 0/1/3/15 on both CIAs: an old zero produces two carries, while an old nonzero produces one. Explicit LOAD and stopped controls remain separate. The earlier new-zero-only rule confused counter history with latch value.

Serial output advances one bit per two Timer-A underflows and asserts SP after sixteen underflows for a clean byte. Serial input still shifts eight external CNT edges. The output half-clock phase, pending automatic reload and old-zero carry are saved, with defaults for older states. Those original serial measurements alone do not establish Timer-B auto-start, electrical IRQ latency or CPU IPL sampling; their separate follow-ups are described above and in the CIA measurement record. Captures, provenance and comparison rules remain in the sibling amiga-hardware-probes repository’s docs/cia-hardware-verification-2026-10-19.md.

Synchronous CPU timer reads sample the reload data mux before the falling edge commits the counter. At an eligible PHI2 reload slot, internal count zero therefore reads as the latch value. This changes readout, not the internal countdown or the continuous period of latch + 1. Counter inspection and fully stopped reads still expose the actual stored count.

These details were isolated with One ROM on the real 512K-chip/512K-slow PAL A500 on 13–14 October 2026: independent reference timers were frozen before reading, live reads were bracketed by a reference STOP, and timer B counted timer-A underflows. A dense 6240-trial live-read sweep fits the same E-clock-calibrated read curve on hardware and Copperline, including 60 hardware reload-boundary samples at latch 15. The calibration offset belongs to the probe’s instruction sequence and is not an emulator timing setting.

The earlier sparse period probe’s reference interval includes setup and reads after the first target sample, so matching that whole interval does not establish matching target-read phase. Use the bracketed reference and large-latch controls for this comparison; raw trial indices and matching NOP counts alone are insufficient.

ICR’s IR bit is a latch: clearing an interrupt mask does not clear a previously recorded enabled interrupt. Reading ICR acknowledges the latch. Re-enabling a still-pending source arms the IRQ pin even when IR was already set. The mask-clear captures constrain this readback behavior, rather than the electrical pin’s precise deassertion edge.

The hardware probe’s CIA measurements include register programming, the timer, interrupt dispatch and VHPOSR readback. Its launch instruction/E-clock phase varies between trials and between boots, so whole-record equality is not an accuracy metric. Compare phase-matched samples or elapsed-time distributions, and exclude ambiguous VHPOSR reads at horizontal wrap.

Blitter slowdown and fixed DMA¶

With BLTPRI clear, the slowdown counter follows consecutive denied CPU access slots. A refresh or other fixed-DMA slot still counts while the blitter is in a disabled-channel gap or destination pipeline bubble. A CPU grant resets the counter; an idle blitter phase does not. Fixed DMA retains its priority, and BLTPRI disables this CPU starvation yield.

One ROM measurements on the same A500 compared D-only, descending-fill and line blits with a stopped CPU, normal polling and BLTPRI polling (540 trials). For a 220-by-22-word D-only clear, normal polling measured 9923–9934 colour clocks. Ignoring fixed-DMA misses during idle blitter phases produced 9876–9890; counting them produces 9925–9935. The stopped-CPU and BLTPRI controls are unchanged. These ranges include launch phase, register access and readback time; they are not a constant to add to a blit.

Division bus phase and probe clock conversion¶

The MC68000 divider computes before its final instruction prefetch and IPL sample. Both DIVU and DIVS therefore flush their internal clocks before topping up the prefetch queue; operand reads still precede computation. MULU/MULS keep their separate early-prefetch sequence. One ROM division sweeps vary operands, addressing modes, bitplane count and loop length. A Copper interrupt during the divide confirms that hardware takes the exception before the following marker instruction; the previous early-prefetch sequence could execute that marker first. WinUAE Best Compatibility agrees with the hardware ordering.

The earlier claim that 64 six-plane DIVU operations were 191 colour clocks early was derived from a defective test clock. The horizontal VHPOSR byte already counts colour clocks; multiplying it by two made the computed time jump backwards at a scanline boundary. The corrected test uses 227 * vertical + horizontal. A NOP replaces the redundant doubling so the probe’s own instruction size and cycle cost do not change. Raw beam endpoints and an independent CIA timer cross-check elapsed measurements. Launch phase still matters under display DMA; an isolated table value must not be treated as an operand-independent divider penalty.

The aligned sweep includes seven bitplane counts, eight operand/control kernels, eight launch phases and 1/2/16/64 iterations. All 1,732 unambiguous Copperline samples with the same actual launch beam as hardware have identical elapsed colour clocks. WinUAE has 1,640 such pairs, also identical. In the six-plane, 64-iteration kernels, normal DIVU measures 4763--4767 clocks, worst-case DIVU measures 5102, and DIVS measures 5249--5257 on all three. These totals include the shared loop and observation overhead.

The stopped-CPU event horizon also includes the pending BLIT assertion: the engine can finish its final write while the interrupt countdown still has two clocks left. Bounding idle advances only by engine completion would then sleep past the source assertion even though running-CPU readback was correct.

The native BLIT source phase is modeled three CCK before its INTREQR readback edge, rather than starting the full recognition interval after readback. The synchronized 896-row A500/WinUAE IRQ probe isolates this from identical Copper-generated BLIT/COPER/SOFT controls and RAM/ROM handler placement. After correction the native NOP-phase cases match; EXG-phase cases remain two CCK late. This is an inferred timing model and a documented remaining scheduler/poll-phase question, not a direct capture of Paula’s IPL pins.

The Copper polling row’s fixed beam values overlap after sweeping polling phase; they do not establish an additional fixed Copper interrupt delay.

Beam-register readback¶

Live VHPOSR reads expose a pipelined beam position, not the exact internal counter. Copperline reports the horizontal byte three colour clocks (VHPOSR_LOOKAHEAD_CCK) ahead of the internal Agnus counter at the CPU-visible register-read point, while the first two reported positions of a new line still carry the previous vertical line number. vAmiga models the same hardware quirk as a five-cycle lead at its peek point; Copperline’s smaller residual lead accounts for the chip-bus grant already advancing the beam before read_vhposr samples it. The low byte is raw colour clocks, not half clocks. This is visible in timing-test rows 19/20/22/27 and in line-wrap polling loops that read VHPOSR immediately after INTREQR bits become visible.

Real-time pacing¶

Pacing never changes emulated behaviour -- it only decides how much emulated time to advance per host frame and when to sleep. (The one opt-in exception is the fastest-possible JIT speed described below.) Real mode debits a per-frame instruction budget one of two ways, selected by [emulation] pacing_budget:

  • cycles (default): the budget is debited by the chip-bus (device) time each slice actually consumed. The cycle-exact core advances the chipset through every CPU cycle as it executes -- internal cycles, bus-cycle tails, chip-bus grants and contention waits -- so the slice’s elapsed bus CCK is the true hardware cost (real_slice_accounting in src/emulator.rs). Because the m68k core’s 68000 cycle totals are exact across its SingleStepTests validation corpus, this matches a stock PAL 68000.

  • instructions: a flat cycles-per-instruction quota (COPPERLINE_REAL_CPU_CPI, default 4.0), debited by retired instructions -- cheaper and pacing-robust, but runs the CPU faster than hardware for instruction mixes above the flat cost.

COPPERLINE_REAL_PACING_BUDGET=cycles|instructions overrides the config for one run; the config overrides the built-in cycles default. COPPERLINE_REAL_PACING_PROFILE=1 emits a one-second pacing log (see Peripherals and expansion). Accelerated CPUs scale the budget by cpu_clocks_per_cck; fast RAM/ROM access costs are scaled with sub-CCK carry accumulation so fractional costs are not lost.

Warp (Emulator::set_paced(false)) skips the sleep entirely. While the window sees the operator holding a host key or a button on the guest’s mouse, and for a short grace period after (WARP_INPUT_YIELD_GRACE), it engages Emulator::set_warp_input_yield, which re-anchors the pacing clock and keeps the sleep in force underneath warp until released, so a physical hold or the gap between two clicks lasts as long in emulated time as it did in the hand. paced() keeps reporting warp as engaged throughout; only the pacer’s sleep decision (paces_realtime) changes. Scheduled and control-protocol input never engages it.

cycles became the default once the core’s 68000 cycle counts were made accurate: under flat instruction pacing, or with the old static cycle counts, a blitter-bound chip-RAM scene had the CPU over-issuing chip-bus accesses and starving the line blitter. With accurate per-instruction costs the CPU’s chip-bus slot ratio settles at a physically valid value naturally, which (together with the area-fill C-slot fix) resolved that blanking regression.

Fastest-possible JIT speed¶

[cpu] speed = "fastest" (JIT only) is the one setting where pacing does change emulated behaviour, and it is opt-in and documented as not reproducible. WinUAE’s Fastest possible keeps the chipset in real time and gives the CPU whatever host time each frame leaves; Copperline does the same with an adaptive JIT clock and spare-time CPU bursts (Fastest possible ([cpu] speed = "fastest")). Each paced quantum first advances the machine at the current clock. step_real_until then fixes a deadline 10% of the quantum (between 200 microseconds and 2 milliseconds) before the presentation target and runs bursts until then, so bus progress during the bursts cannot extend it. Precise stepping, reverse debugging and run-ahead never enter the burst phase.

SpeedGovernor in src/emulator/fastest.rs chooses the regular clock:

  • After every real-time quantum step_real_until reports the host time it spent on regular emulation (bursts and pacing sleep excluded), the host time since the previous quantum ended (the frontend’s share of the main thread), the emulated time advanced, the instructions retired, and whether the JIT batch path ran. Time the window spends presenting a frame on the main thread (the RTG, inspector and screenshot paths, which can block on vsync) is reported through Emulator::note_frontend_wait and left out of the frontend share: it is a wait, not work, and frames the present worker takes never touch the main thread at all.

  • Every 0.1 s of emulated or host time, whichever comes first, the governor computes the load: host time over emulated time, 1.0 when the host only just keeps up. Closing on host time as well bounds how long an overloaded clock survives in wall time.

  • Above 0.93 it lowers the clock and below 0.80 it raises it, each time by the square root of 0.85 / load: at most a 4x cut or a 1.25x rise per window. A full proportional step overshoots near saturation, where frame cost climbs faster than the clock, and made the clock hunt between two values in measurements; half the log-gain settles. Between the two thresholds it holds, so measurement noise does not make it hunt. Host cost grows less than in proportion to the clock (the chipset’s work per emulated second is fixed), so the steps approach the band from below.

  • It only raises the clock when the CPU is clock-bound: the regular quantum retired at least a quarter of an instruction per billed clock. A CPU idling in STOP or starved by chip-bus waits gains nothing from a faster clock, and ramping it would only cause a hiccup when the workload changes. Burst instructions are left out of this test, so a CPU that is busy only in its bursts does not raise the regular clock.

  • The clock stays between clock_mhz and 2000 MHz. The floor matters when chipset or frontend work alone overloads the host: lowering the CPU cannot cure that, and would leave an idle or bus-bound guest at a clock it never qualifies to raise again.

  • A gap of more than 100 ms between quanta (a paused machine, a modal dialog, a debugger break) discards the window, as does any quantum stepped on the precise core (debug hooks armed, a run-to-PC request). reanchor_realtime_clock discards it too.

  • A save-state load rebases the floor to the state’s clock. A governor that has never moved the clock follows the new floor exactly, so an unpaced run that loads a state still matches speed = "fixed"; one that has adapted keeps its clock within the new bounds.

  • Warp runs unpaced at the last measured throughput (bursts included) as a fixed clock, never below clock_mhz. Other unpaced quanta (run-ahead bursts, headless runs) discard the window and leave the clock where it is. A headless run therefore never leaves clock_mhz and is byte-identical to speed = "fixed", unless COPPERLINE_HEADLESS_PACED=1 paces it like the window to measure exactly this.

Measuring wall time rather than CPU time makes the controller back off when other processes compete for the core. It also copes with hosts whose scheduler parks a lightly loaded thread on efficiency cores at a low clock (macOS does): the paced thread looks expensive at first, moves to a performance core as the load rises, its cost per instruction falls, and the governor raises the clock further.

The performance overlay’s cpu N MHz and the control protocol’s jit_mhz report measured throughput rather than the regular clock: retired instructions per emulated second, bursts included, expressed as an equivalent one-instruction-per-clock rate.

Thread scheduling priority¶

Pacing only works if the host actually runs the right thread at the right moment. Two threads are latency-critical: the pacer (the main thread, which advances the core and calls thread::sleep in Emulator::sleep_until_realtime_device_time) and the cpal audio callback (which drains the sample ring buffer the pacer keeps ~150 ms ahead of the device clock). During live-audio startup and rebuffering, the pacer treats the unfilled prebuffer as additional required lead; the large-stall self-heal allows for that lead so it does not cancel the refill as if it were a host pause. The copperline-render and copperline-present workers (Architecture overview) are throughput threads, not latency ones, and are left at normal priority. When the host is busy, a scheduler that preempts the pacer shows up as frame stutter, and one that preempts the audio callback shows up as an audible underrun.

[emulation] realtime_priority (off by default; COPPERLINE_REALTIME_PRIORITY overrides it for one run) asks the OS to schedule those two threads above normal. It is best effort -- src/priority.rs logs what it did and never fails the run -- and, like all pacing, it never changes emulated behaviour; it only changes when host work is scheduled. The implementation is per-platform because “real-time priority” is portable in neither API nor semantics:

  • macOS -- the pacer thread joins the USER_INTERACTIVE QoS class (pthread_set_qos_class_self_np), the idiomatic unprivileged low-latency request. The audio callback is left untouched: Core Audio already runs it on a real-time thread, and pinning a QoS class onto it would only demote it.

  • Windows -- both threads are raised to THREAD_PRIORITY_HIGHEST via the thread-priority crate; no privilege required.

  • Linux / other Unix -- raising priority needs privilege (an rtprio rlimit, CAP_SYS_NICE, or root); without it the request is logged and declined, and the thread keeps normal scheduling.

The pacer sleeps between work chunks rather than spinning, so even the strongest scheduling class it can land in still yields the CPU and cannot starve the host -- which is why elevating it is safe to offer.

Cross-checking against hardware¶

timing-test/ is a bootable disk that measures CPU and chip-bus operation timings against the CIA E-clock and reports them on screen and over serial. The same numbers can be collected from Copperline, vAmiga, FS-UAE, and real Amigas; several timing fixes (IRQ latency, the area-fill C slot) were validated this way. When changing the timing model, update the corresponding reference doc and add a named regression test for the hardware behaviour.

Behaviours with a natural on-screen form are also pinned by golden probe renders. Each probe is a bootblock program in timing-test/ (the .asm source committed beside its assembled .bin) that takes over the machine and draws the behaviour as a static display: bars, strips, or marker positions. tests/probe_golden.rs wraps each .bin in a bootable ADF, boots it on the bundled AROS ROM, and compares a screenshot pixel for pixel with timing-test/golden/<name>.png; the probes named in this chapter (ddfprobe-ddfmiss, ddfprobe-sotb, hamprobe-select, copprobe-jmpread, ...) are among them. The suite runs only in release builds. After an intentional change, re-bless the goldens and review the render diff:

COPPERLINE_BLESS_GOLDEN=1 cargo test --release --test probe_golden

The two probes that display live E-clock-referenced counts (timing-test itself and bltprobe-pace) also move whenever the bundled AROS ROM is refreshed, because their phase depends on how long the ROM takes to boot. a500-cia-request and jit-bus-boundary can move for the same reason. timing-test/README.md (“CI golden renders”) has the details, including the vAmiga cross-checks behind individual probes.

A500 review boundary regressions¶

The a500-blit-boundaries and a500-display-boundaries golden probes retain raw startup, BBUSY/BZERO, descending destination-restore, collision-clear, pointer-write and line-end-refresh samples. The display probe pairs single and dual playfields. They supplement the versioned numerical hardware captures; rendered emulator goldens are not hardware measurements.

The destination-restore measurements require classic CPU RAM writes to land at each granted word transfer, before DMA runs in the bus cycle’s free tail. Committing the complete write after that tail reverses CPU/blitter ordering despite apparently correct grants. The same transfer boundary is used for byte, word and longword writes, including separate classic longword halves.

CIA mask writes do not acknowledge an already-latched interrupt: IR and the external request remain asserted until an ICR read. One-shot START readback can show STOP during the E-high transfer before falling-edge decrement/underflow commit; this preview does not apply during the LOAD hold. Internal carry and timer-A-to-timer-B cascade state are preserved. These constraints come from paired measurements of both CIAs, with explicit acknowledge, masked-source, zero/one/nonzero-latch and timing-gap controls in amiga-hardware-probes.
